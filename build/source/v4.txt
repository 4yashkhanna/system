T: THE SYSTEM IN SENTENCES (FOR PRESENTING)
T: The festival grows through several engines at once. A bigger celebration becomes more visible, which brings visitors and donations that fund the next one (R4). Expectations of a good festival rise with it and push a more elaborate theme and the space it needs (R3), decided on site by the trust leadership (D07), seemingly without access and traffic being part of that decision. The famous central mandals pull trips from every neighbourhood (R5), and the procession draws mandals through prestige until one day of police capacity caps it (R2 · R27 · B2). State grants, patrons and election season add money from outside. Locally, attachment keeps pandals going (R14) and small pandals multiply because each one makes the next easier (R8 · B4). Sites that worked are carried over with their permissions (R9, R21), layouts are reused until the footprint spills onto paths (R22 · B22), and a visible main-road spot pulls crowds that lock in its closures (R23 · B23). On the street, more space taken and more people raise congestion. Barricades channel the crowd and clear the site (B5) and compact it into dedicated space (B24), but diversions push congestion onto surrounding routes and side roads, pulling police to new hotspots (R11, R24). Drivers divert to save time (B25) and visitors steer around jams using the metro and advisories (R7 · B3, B26), until everyone takes the same detour (R13). Volunteers guide crowds, yet because they act as the signage nobody invests in signs, and their workload caps what they can do (R1 · B1). Congestion and blocked junctions slow ambulances, which may lead to kept-clear corridors (B6, B27). Hawkers who are moved on may set up at the crowd's edge, though our evidence is mixed (R10). The costs land on people who did not choose them. Most residents and delivery workers adapt privately, rerouting trips and errands (B19, B20), which keeps the disruption out of sight, so arrangements stay the same (R15, R29). When it becomes an organised complaint, as with the Raheja Reserve memo on dhol-tasha rehearsals, it reaches planners and pushes back on space, noise and enforcement (B9, B10, B12, B33, R16 · B14). The belief that complaining would be disrespectful, and routines learned over the years (B16), tilt the system towards silence. Permissions run through the familiar police chowki, where informal nods keep it the front door, while the single window and formal checks close the rule gap (R26 · B29). Clashes on the ground push joint plans and build trust (R25 · B28, B15), but agencies still plan one stage at a time and firefight at each (R18 · B17); one plan for the whole cycle is the balancing response (B30). Police go where crowds have been (R28), and borrowed staff stretch routine services until they are pulled back (B31 · B32), so the festival is handled without new systems and the case for dedicated capacity never builds (R17). After visarjan the festival ends for participants but not for the system: crews close the gap to a restored street slowly, against a three-day rule that slips to weeks (B13), and crews booked beyond those available push teardown later (R20 · B21). Immersion is the one area where rules visibly changed practice (B8), and it moves community tasks to PMC (R19 · B18). Whether any of this becomes a lesson for next year depends on problems being seen and recorded (B11, B34), and is still the biggest unknown on the map.
